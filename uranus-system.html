Because Uranus lies on its side, a pole points almost straight at the Sun for half of the 84-year orbit. Averaging the daily insolation Q = (S₀/π)[H₀ sinφ sinδ + cosφ cosδ sinH₀] over a full orbit gives the counter-intuitive result: above a critical tilt of 54.7°, the poles receive more annual sunlight than the equator. Drag the slider from 0° (poles cold, equator hot — a low-tilt world) up past 54.7° to Uranus's 97.8° and watch the curve invert. Every latitude poleward of ~7.8° sees the midnight Sun at some point in the Uranian year, and each pole gets ≈42 years of continuous daylight followed by ≈42 years of night.

Miranda's wild surface — fault-bounded coronae and the ~20 km cliff Verona Rupes, the tallest known anywhere — needs an internal heat source the tiny moon shouldn't have today. The leading idea: long ago Miranda passed through a 3:1 mean-motion resonance with Umbriel that pumped its eccentricity and inclination. Tidal heating scales as Ė ∝ (k₂/Q) e², so even a modest forced e during resonance dumps far more heat than today's nearly circular orbit. The system later escaped, damping the eccentricity but leaving the 4.34° inclination frozen in — the fossil that gives the game away (Tittemore & Wisdom 1990; Hammond & Barr 2014). Gravity ⇄ rings. Uranus's J₂ and J₄ were not measured by a probe — they were inferred from the rings. Each narrow ring is a precessing ellipse whose apsidal/nodal rate is set by the gravity harmonics, so timing the rings in stellar occultations (French et al. 1988; Jacobson 2014) inverts to the field. Shepherds. The ε ring's edges are held by Cordelia (24:25 resonance, inner) and Ophelia (14:13, outer); Cressida's mass was weighed through its resonance with the η ring. Magnetosphere ⇄ moons. Why Uranus is the strangest planet

A world knocked on its side. Uranus's 97.77° obliquity tips it just past the pole, so it rotates retrograde and effectively rolls around the Sun. The leading explanation is one or more giant impacts early in its history. The tilt drives everything you see here: the rings and regular moons orbit in Uranus's equatorial plane, which now stands almost perpendicular to its orbital plane, while the captured irregulars stay near the orbital plane — so the two families occupy visibly different planes.

Miranda, the shattered moon. Despite being only ~470 km across, Miranda has the most extreme terrain known: grooved ovoid coronae and Verona Rupes, a ~20 km scarp. Its surface and its anomalous 4.34° inclination are best explained by a past mean-motion resonance with Umbriel that pumped its orbit and heated its interior before the system escaped the resonance.

The rings, the shepherds, and the lopsided magnet

Found by a flicker. Uranus's rings were discovered in 1977 when a background star blinked out several times on either side of the planet during an occultation — the second ring system ever found. They are extremely narrow, sharp-edged and charcoal-dark. The brightest, the ε ring, is slightly eccentric and is pinned between two shepherd moons, Cordelia (inner) and Ophelia (outer); the faint outer μ ring is dust knocked off the moon Mab.

The most crowded moons known. A dozen small moons are packed into a narrow band just outside the rings. They perturb one another so strongly that the system is dynamically unstable on million-year timescales — Cressida and Desdemona are on track to collide. Press ▶ Play and watch the inner-moon dynamics plot to follow their forced eccentricities and shrinking separation.

A wildly tilted magnet. Uranus's magnetic dipole is inclined ~58.6° to its spin axis and offset ~0.31 R_U from the planet's centre. Combined with the 98° obliquity, the magnetosphere tumbles dramatically through each rotation — unlike any other planet.
