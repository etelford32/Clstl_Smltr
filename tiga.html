    A ground magnetometer records two physically separate planets at once, and each one's signal is the other's noise. Below about a cycle per decade the record is the geodynamo — currents in liquid iron 2,891 km down. Above it, the record is the magnetosphere. Field modellers subtract the second to see the first; space-weather modellers subtract the first to see the second.

    Pick a layer below. The view above is one Earth seen three ways — the camera flies, the shells cross-fade, and the panel changes with it. Only the top layer is real-time, and it is a nowcast with zero lead time, not a forecast. Nothing here is a geodynamo simulation: what is drawn is the observed field continued down to the core, field lines integrated through it, and magnetic diffusion actually solved. The missing piece is the flow — and that is the piece that needs a supercomputer.

Hemispheric balance, not station count, sets the amplitude — and reselecting six of eleven every month changes both the amplitude and the UT phase of a bias reaching tens of nT during a large storm.

    Dst is TIGA with one parameter

    Write out the degree-1 expansion, keep only the zonal term, and Xi/cos λi = −(q₁⁰+g₁⁰) identically at every station. Averaging and dividing by mean cos λ — the Dst and SYM-H recipe exactly — is the least-squares estimator of that coefficient.

    So the distinction is not estimator-versus-index. Everything here is an estimator; they differ in how many parameters they fit, how they weight stations, and whether they carry a prior. Dst is the one-parameter, equal-weight, no-memory degenerate case — and that is why a Bayesian version has a posterior to publish, which neither Kyoto nor USGS emits.

    The frequency split — and why the two communities need each other

    A core signal of period T must diffuse outward through 2,891 km of conducting mantle. This is the transfer function, and it is why nothing fast gets through.

    σ = 0.1 S/m σ = 1 S/m σ = 10 S/m
    σ (S/m)50% attenuation ata 1-day signal arrives as
    At σ = 1 S/m a one-day core signal reaches the surface with 3×10⁻⁹ of its amplitude. Daily core analysis is not a data problem; no instrument fixes it.

    The logic runs backwards too, and that half is better: we do observe jerks at 1–3 years and torsional waves at ~6 years, so the mantle cannot be as conductive as the uniform σ = 10 case. The observations bound the interior.
    And this is why the three layers belong on one page. Core signals at short period are ~1–3 nT. Sq is 30 nT, storm recovery 50, main phase 100. The core signals we most want are buried an order of magnitude under external noise — so detecting them is an external-field-removal problem. The better the magnetosphere is modelled, the deeper into the Earth we can see.
    The view above is now showing the core. Radii are true — Earth 1.0, core–mantle boundary 0.546, inner core 0.192, nothing compressed. Pull the mantle down to see inside, and the truncation slider down to watch the small-scale structure — and its uncertainty — disappear together.

    This is not a geodynamo simulation, and nothing on this page claims to be one. A real 3-D MHD dynamo needs a supercomputer, and the best published runs still sit eight orders of magnitude from Earth's Ekman number — you can read that number off the table below. What runs here are reduced models, small enough for a browser tab precisely because they throw away almost all of the physics. They still answer real questions.

    Only one layer can run a dynamo — and not the one you would guess

    Switch the view above to Layer structure and pull the cutaway open. Each shell carries its own conductivity, its own diffusion time, and its own answer to whether a dynamo could operate in it.

    The inner core is exactly as conductive as the outer core. It still cannot host a dynamo, because Rm = UL/η is a product — and a solid layer contributes U = 0 however conductive it is. The outer core is the only shell that is both conducting and moving. Conductivity is necessary; motion is what is scarce.

    The inner core is not idle, though: field can only diffuse through it, on a — timescale. That is slow enough to resist a rapid reversal of the field around it, which is the basis of the published idea that the inner core stabilises reversals.

    The Taylor–Proudman theorem: when Coriolis overwhelms inertia — and at Rossby ≈ 10−6 it overwhelmingly does — flow becomes invariant along the rotation axis and organises into columns. A column that would have to pass through the solid inner core cannot exist, so the fluid splits in two:

    Outside the cylinder, columnar rolls, made helical by Coriolis with opposite handedness in each hemisphere — which is the antisymmetric α-effect that the controlled experiment below shows can prefer a dipole. Inside, the polar regions, largely decoupled.

    So “rotation selects the dipole” is not a slogan. It is this geometry. Remove the rotation and you keep a magnetic field but lose the axial dipole.
    Downward continuation is real, and it is where over-interpretation starts. The expansion is valid anywhere the field is current-free, so evaluating it at the CMB is legitimate — but each degree gains (a/r)n+2, about 6× at degree 1 and 8,700× at degree 13. Continuation amplifies the coefficients' own error along with the signal, and past degree 13 it would amplify the crustal field as though it came from the core.

    Switch the dynamo off Solved, not sketched

    This runs the induction equation with the flow set to zero — ∂B/∂t = η∇2B — as a real diffusion solve on the core, spectral in angle and implicit in radius. It is checkable: the decay rates it produces match the analytic eigenvalues τn = μ0σa2/kn2 to 0.03%. Drag the clock forward and watch which multipole is left.

    Why the field is a dipole

    Switch the dynamo off and each multipole decays ohmically at its own rate, τn = μ₀σa²/kn².

    degree nfirst zero of jnfree-decay timevs dipole
    Three reasons, in increasing order of interest. (1) ∇·B = 0 forbids a monopole, so degree 1 is the lowest multipole available — a constraint of Maxwell's equations, not a preference. (2) The dipole survives — longer than degree 13, so the dynamo works least hard to sustain it. (3) Rotation selects it — but only inside a window, which is the interesting one.

    The controlled experiment: why a dipole, and why axial

    An αΩ mean-field dynamo, solved live. Flip the equatorial symmetry of the α-effect and watch which magnetic parity is preferred. Antisymmetric α is what Coriolis-driven helicity actually gives — opposite handedness in the two hemispheres. Symmetric α is a deliberately unphysical control, and without it "antisymmetric α gives a dipole" would be an observation about one run rather than a result.

    Two findings, and the second is the one worth having. (a) Antisymmetric α can prefer the dipole family; symmetric α never does — it stays mixed or frankly quadrupolar at every dynamo number. (b) But the dipole preference holds only for |D| between roughly 116 and 1166 — one decade. Weaker or stronger driving both give a quadrupole.

    A dipole is therefore not inevitable. It requires the right symmetry and the right strength, and Earth happens to sit in the window. That is the same message as the dipolar/multipolar transition found in full 3-D simulations, reached here from a model that runs in a second.

    Chron lengths spread by a factor of several with no periodicity. Reversal is what a nonlinear dynamo does unprompted — a statement about dynamos, not a forecast. This page does not predict reversals and nothing here could.

    It is also chaotic, so the exact reversal count is not portable between integrators. The statistics are; the count is not, and quoting one to four digits would be quoting an artefact of a solver.

    Nothing down there is a magnet

    Layer structure, conductivity, and the temperatures that settle the question.

    Layerdepth (km)σ (S/m)state
    Curie temperatures are 858 K for magnetite, 948 K for hematite, 1043 K for iron. The core runs at 5000–6000 K and the lower mantle above 2000 K. Every deep layer is far above its Curie point, so the field is carried entirely by electric currents — roughly 10⁹ A — and not by magnetised rock. The only magnetised rock on Earth is the thin cold crust, which is precisely why the Lowes spectrum flattens above degree 13.

    And the field's shape is not electromagnetic at all: Coriolis → columnar flow → helicity of opposite sign per hemisphere → antisymmetric α → dipole family. The magnetism is electromagnetic; the shape is rotational.

    What this is, said precisely

    TIGA is a sequential Bayesian state estimator: a Kalman filter whose state is the degree-1 external Gauss coefficients (q₁⁰, q₁¹, s₁¹) plus their rates, updated each minute from whichever observatories reported. It is not a simulation — nothing is integrated forward physically; the model is an integrated random walk, which is a statistical prior, not dynamics. It is not a forecast — zero lead time. It does not compete with L1-driven forecast models; it is what you have when L1 is unavailable.

    Falsified predictions, kept in the record

    Two pre-registered predictions did not survive. Excluding all eleven canonical stations was predicted to hurt badly and did not (three parameters are already over-determined by 64 stations). And the sector-loss penalty was predicted to decay with time as Earth's rotation carried surviving stations through the vacated local time — it does decay, but a memoryless control decays identically, so the decay is the storm, not the memory. Both are recorded rather than quietly dropped, because they were the most informative results.
