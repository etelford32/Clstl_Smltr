Field note · Forecast verification

NOAA said G1. Earth got G3. The variable the models can't see.

On July 4, 2026, a geomagnetic storm arrived two full levels above forecast and put aurora over more than thirty states. The solar wind was unremarkable. One number did all the work — and it is the one number operational CME models cannot produce.

Parkers Physics · July 25, 2026 · 9 min read

Aurora was photographed from New Mexico. From Northern California. From driveways belonging to people who had never seen it before, on a holiday weekend, with no warning worth the name.

The forecast for that night was G1 — Minor. The lowest rung on the NOAA scale. What arrived was G3 — Strong.

The easy conclusion is that somebody got it wrong. That conclusion is incorrect, and the correct one is more interesting: the forecast was the honest output of a model class that is structurally incapable of predicting the variable that decided the outcome.

What happened on July 4, 2026?

The storm began four days earlier and 150 million kilometres away.

On June 29, three active regions — AR4475, AR4478 and AR4479 — simultaneously carried beta-gamma-delta magnetic classifications. That is the most complex configuration in the standard sunspot taxonomy and the signature of a region storing enough free magnetic energy to produce X-class flares.

On June 30, AR4479 delivered. An X1.1 flare peaked at 20:50 UTC, triggered an R3 radio blackout across the sunlit hemisphere, and launched a halo coronal mass ejection at roughly 1,500 km/s.

Forecasters took it seriously. The CME was tracked and modelled, and NOAA carried an active analysis of possible Earth impact through July 3 and 4. The arrival call was broadly right.

The shock reached L1 early on July 4. Per the SIDC daily bulletin, total interplanetary field strength climbed to about 24 nT at 00:33 UTC. Solar wind speed settled between roughly 550 and 640 km/s. And at 01:05 UTC, the north–south component of the field — Bz — reached a minimum of about −19 nT.

Conditions ran G1 to G3 across the day. Around 09:30 UTC, Bz rotated northward and turned weakly positive. The storm subsided.

The detail almost every writeup missed

Look at the solar wind speed again: 550 to 640 km/s.

That is not fast. It is barely above what a decent coronal hole delivers on an ordinary week. The May 2024 Gannon superstorm drove speeds well north of 800 km/s. The January 2026 radiation storm rode a CME clocked near 1,700 km/s in transit.

By the usual intuition — faster CME, bigger storm — July 4 should have been forgettable. The kinetic energy arriving at the magnetosphere was moderate.

It was a strong storm anyway, because geomagnetic storm intensity is not primarily set by how hard the solar wind hits. It is set by how efficiently the solar wind's magnetic field couples to Earth's.

What is Bz, and why does it decide storm strength?

Earth's magnetic field points north at the equator. When the arriving interplanetary field points south — negative Bz — the two fields are antiparallel at the dayside boundary, and they reconnect.

Reconnection is the door. It is the mechanism that lets solar wind energy into the magnetosphere at all, and it opens in proportion to how strongly and how persistently that field points south. When Bz points north, the door stays mostly shut.

A 1,500 km/s CME with a northward field can produce a disappointing storm. A moderate CME with a strongly southward field can produce a memorable one.

The quantitative version has been in the literature since 1987, when Gonzalez and Tsurutani established that Bz at or below −10 nT sustained for more than three hours is sufficient to drive an intense geomagnetic storm.

July 4 hit −19 nT — roughly twice the intense-storm threshold — and held southward for hours before rotating. The magnetosphere did not get hit hard. It got hit open.

Notice how the storm ended, too. It did not decay because the driver ran out of energy; solar wind speed was still elevated. It ended when the flux rope rotated and Bz went positive. Orientation started the storm and orientation ended it. You can watch that coupling run against live conditions in our ring current simulation.

Why did the forecast say G1?

Here is the structural problem, and it is no secret in the field.

The workhorse operational model for CME propagation is WSA-ENLIL with a cone geometry input. It is a genuinely good model. It solves magnetohydrodynamics through the inner heliosphere and predicts arrival time and arrival speed with real skill.

But the cone model represents a CME as a hydrodynamic pressure pulse — a blob with a width, a direction and a speed. It carries no internal magnetic flux rope. There is no coherent twisted field in the input, so there is no Bz to propagate, so there is no Bz in the output.

The model cannot forecast Bz because the model does not contain Bz.

So what does an operational Kp forecast rest on? Largely speed, density and shock strength, mapped statistically onto historical storm response. That mapping is reasonable on average. It was exactly wrong for July 4 — a moderate-speed CME whose flux rope happened to arrive with near-ideal southward alignment. The statistics said G1 because moderate CMEs usually give G1. This one did not.

The forecast was not wrong about the CME. It was silent about the flux rope, and the flux rope decided everything.

Doesn't L1 solve this?

We do measure Bz — continuously, from the L1 Lagrange point about 1.5 million kilometres upstream, now via NOAA's SOLAR-1 spacecraft, which took over primary solar wind monitoring from DSCOVR in mid-2026.

But L1 sits about one percent of the way to the Sun. At typical solar wind speeds that buys 15 to 60 minutes of warning.

Fifteen to sixty minutes is enough to confirm a storm is happening. It is not enough to reposition a spacecraft, reconfigure a grid, or reroute a polar flight — and it is far too late to revise a public forecast issued two days earlier.

L1 tells you the answer as the test is being graded. It does not help you study.

Can Bz be forecast in advance?

Not deterministically. But "not deterministically" is a long way from "not at all," and the entire useful range lives in that gap.

A CME's flux rope is not assembled at random in transit. It erupts from a specific source region with a specific magnetic topology, and magnetic helicity is approximately conserved on the way out. That means the orientation and handedness of the arriving field are constrained by things you can observe on the Sun days ahead:

  • The orientation of the polarity inversion line in the source active region
  • The chirality of the pre-eruption filament or sigmoid
  • The axis of the post-eruption arcade
  • The surrounding coronal field, which torques the rope as it leaves

From those you can build a prior on the arriving axial field direction — not a certainty, but a distribution. Propagate that rope through the heliosphere with its magnetic structure intact rather than as a pressure blob. Let it rotate and deflect in transit, because it does. Then run the ensemble.

The output is not a single Kp number. It is a probability: most likely G1, with a 25% tail at G3 or above.

For a satellite operator, an aurora chaser or a grid engineer, those are not the same forecast — even though the most likely value is identical. A point forecast of G1 told people to stay home on July 4. A probabilistic forecast would have told them the tail was worth watching.

What we're changing

To be direct about the limits: source-region Bz prediction is a research frontier, not a solved problem, and skill scores are modest. Anyone advertising deterministic multi-day Bz forecasting is overselling. Here is what we are actually building.

A standalone flux rope engine. Rather than bolting magnetic structure onto an existing simulation as a parameter, we built it as its own model with its own page — the Flux Rope Simulator, a reusable core that feeds the solar simulations, drives the ring current model, and plugs into the live space weather dashboard for real-time correction as an event unfolds. If magnetic structure is the missing variable, it deserves to be a first-class object in the codebase.

Ensemble output instead of point output. Every forecast surface we ship should carry its uncertainty. A single number implies a confidence the physics does not support. AurOracle is where that shows up first for aurora chasers.

Inner-magnetosphere coupling. Getting Bz right upstream only matters if the downstream chain converts it faithfully into ring current injection and ground-level disturbance. Our Gannon hindcast made that lesson concrete: the index everyone uses for satellite drag saturates exactly when you need it most. Both halves of the chain have to work.

Event post-mortems as standing practice. Every significant event gets this treatment: what was forecast, what arrived, where our own model diverged, and what changed as a result — published whether or not it flatters us. A model you cannot audit is a model you should not trust. See also our St. Patrick's Day 2015 replay.

The part that should worry you

Solar Cycle 25 peaked in October 2024. We are in the declining phase, which sounds like the calm part and historically is not — the strongest events of a cycle tend to cluster after maximum. The Halloween 2003 superstorms landed two years into Cycle 23's decline. Cycle 25 is an odd-numbered cycle, and extreme storms in odd cycles skew later still.

July 4 was a G3 forecast as a G1, driven by a moderate CME. The same failure mode applied to a fast CME with a well-aligned rope is not a surprise aurora over New Mexico. It is a surprise that arrives at a grid operator with no useful warning.

That is the gap worth closing, and it is the one we are working on.

Frequently asked questions

Why was the July 4, 2026 storm stronger than forecast?

Because storm strength is set by the orientation of the arriving magnetic field, not the speed of the CME. Solar wind speed peaked around 640 km/s — moderate — but Bz reached −19 nT and stayed southward for hours, driving efficient reconnection. Operational CME models predict speed and arrival time but carry no internal magnetic structure, so they could not anticipate the orientation.

What is Bz in space weather?

Bz is the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field carried by the solar wind. Negative (southward) Bz is antiparallel to Earth's field at the dayside boundary and allows magnetic reconnection, which is how solar wind energy enters the magnetosphere. Sustained Bz at or below −10 nT for more than three hours is the classic threshold for an intense geomagnetic storm.

How much warning does the L1 satellite give?

Roughly 15 to 60 minutes, depending on solar wind speed. L1 sits about 1.5 million kilometres upstream — about one percent of the distance to the Sun. That is enough to confirm a storm is beginning but not enough to revise a forecast issued days earlier or to complete most protective operational measures.

Can aurora forecasts be improved?

Yes, but not by making point forecasts more precise. The realistic path is probabilistic: estimate the erupting flux rope's orientation from source-region magnetic topology, propagate it with its magnetic structure intact, and publish a distribution of outcomes rather than a single Kp value.
