The Gap

Three storms. Three misses. This is the regime you’re judged on.

Storm-time drag isn’t the edge case — it’s where fleets are lost, conjunction screens go stale, and reentry windows slip. Quiet-day forecasts are easy. The tail is the job.

Feb 2022 · Starlink

The forecast said the air was thin

A modest G1–G2 storm raised thermospheric density well past the empirical call. 38 newly-launched Starlink satellites couldn’t raise orbit against the drag and reentered. The model wasn’t wrong about quiet time — it was wrong about the storm.

May 2024 · Gannon G5

The strongest storm in two decades

The Gannon superstorm drove mass density spikes that empirical models smoothed away. It is the primary validation and fleet stress-test case behind the product’s storm-time approach.

2024 · AR3842

Back-to-back X-class flares

An active region produced repeated strong flares and CMEs, compressing forecast lead time. Operators needed the drag multiplier before the event, not after — the difference between a planned maneuver and a scramble.

What “Holds Up” Means

Physics, not a black box.

Empirical models fit a climatology to history. They’re fast and fine on a calm day — and they revert to the mean exactly when the storm pushes the atmosphere off the curve.

Empirical density (NRLMSISE / JB-class)

  • Fit to historical climatology
  • Reverts to mean during storms
  • Underforecasts storm-time drag 2–5×
  • No physical driver chain to audit

Parkers — MHD-grounded

  • SWMF / BATS-R-US magnetosphere coupling
  • Storm energy deposition drives density
  • Exposes the storm-energy driver chain
  • Every number traceable to the equations
Where we’re honest: on a quiet day, our forecast and a good empirical model are roughly a wash — the thermosphere is on its climatological curve and there’s little physics to add. The target edge is the tail: the storm-time regime where the empirical assumption breaks and your fleet is most exposed. Predictive-accuracy claims stay behind the published hindcast validation gate.
